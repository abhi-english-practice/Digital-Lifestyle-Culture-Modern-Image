Reading 01
FOMO
Digital Well-being

The term FOMO, short for Fear of Missing Out, is commonly used to describe the uneasy feeling that other people may be having rewarding experiences from which they are absent, and that they are therefore being left behind socially, emotionally, or even professionally. Although it is often associated with social media, the concept has broader psychological significance because constant exposure to curated images of success, enjoyment, and connection can intensify comparison and dissatisfaction. In this sense, FOMO is not simply about curiosity or interest in what others are doing. It is more accurately understood as a form of anxiety shaped by the fear that one is missing opportunities, relationships, or experiences that seem important to personal happiness or self-worth.

At the same time, FOMO is not merely a private feeling. Commentators and psychology sources suggest that it can influence behaviour by encouraging compulsive checking, overcommitment, impulsive decisions, and difficulty being satisfied with the present moment. However, its effects can become especially damaging when external visibility is mistaken for genuine fulfillment, and when people begin to measure their own lives against the most carefully curated versions of others' lives. For that reason, FOMO has become an important concept in discussions of social comparison, digital well-being, and the psychological pressure created by always-connected online environments.

JOMO
Digital Well-being

The term JOMO, short for Joy of Missing Out, is commonly used to describe the positive feeling of being content with not participating in every event, trend, or digital demand. It is often presented as a response to FOMO, because instead of fearing exclusion, individuals learn to value presence, rest, and selective attention. In this sense, JOMO is not simply about opting out. It is more accurately understood as a mindset that allows people to appreciate the present rather than measuring it against what might be happening elsewhere.

At the same time, JOMO is not merely a celebration of absence. Commentators and psychology sources suggest that it can help cultivate a healthier relationship with time, social expectations, and digital connection by reducing comparison and encouraging more intentional choices. However, its meaning depends on how it is experienced. If it reflects self-awareness and balance, it may support well-being; if it is driven by avoidance or social withdrawal, it may be more complicated than it first appears. For that reason, JOMO has become an important concept in discussions of mindfulness, digital well-being, and the search for contentment in always-connected environments.

Reading 03
Doomscrolling
Media Behaviour

The term doomscrolling refers to the habit of continuously scrolling through negative or distressing news online, often with a sense that one must remain informed even as the content becomes increasingly overwhelming. Although this behaviour may appear rational in times of crisis, health and psychology sources note that it can quickly shift from information-seeking to compulsive exposure to distressing content. In this sense, doomscrolling is driven by a paradox: people scroll in order to gain control over uncertainty, yet the constant consumption of alarming content often increases anxiety rather than reducing it.

Researchers and clinicians have linked doomscrolling to stress, sleep disruption, reduced well-being, and a heightened sense of threat, especially when individuals repeatedly encounter emotionally charged headlines or violent imagery. The practice does not simply reflect personal weakness or poor self-discipline. It is also shaped by digital environments that reward endless engagement and make it difficult to disengage from negative information. As a result, doomscrolling has become an important concept in discussions of mental health, media overload, and the psychological costs of life online.

Reading 04
Joy Scrolling
Media Behaviour

The term joy scrolling is commonly used to describe the intentional habit of scrolling through uplifting, amusing, or inspiring online content in order to cultivate a more positive emotional experience. It is often presented as a contrast to doomscrolling, since the goal is not to consume distressing headlines but to seek out moments of humour, creativity, encouragement, or relief. In this sense, joy scrolling reflects a more selective approach to digital media: rather than passively consuming whatever appears, users try to shape their online environment toward content that feels restorative or emotionally lighter.

At the same time, joy scrolling is not simply about entertainment. Commentators and educators have suggested that it can function as a small form of emotional self-management, especially in periods when online spaces feel saturated with anxiety, conflict, or distressing content. However, its value depends on how it is used. If it becomes only a form of avoidance, it may offer temporary comfort without addressing deeper stress. For that reason, joy scrolling is best understood as an attempt to balance digital consumption by making room for content that supports well-being, curiosity, and a healthier relationship with life online.

De-Influencing
Consumer Culture

The term de-influencing is commonly used to describe the practice of advising audiences not to buy certain products, especially those that are heavily promoted, overpriced, or unlikely to deliver the results advertised. It is often presented as a reaction against influencer culture and the consumer pressure generated by relentless online promotion. In this sense, de-influencing reflects a more critical approach to digital consumption: rather than encouraging people to follow trends automatically, it invites them to question whether particular purchases are necessary, worthwhile, or aligned with their actual needs.

At the same time, de-influencing is not simply the opposite of influencing. Commentators have noted that it can still shape desire, redirect attention, and even generate new forms of consumer behaviour by telling audiences what to avoid and, sometimes, what to choose instead. This makes the concept especially interesting, because it combines scepticism toward overconsumption with the continued power of online persuasion. As a result, de-influencing has become an important term in discussions of authenticity, mindful spending, and the changing relationship between social media creators and consumer trust.

Hustle Culture
Work & Identity

The term hustle culture is commonly used to describe a mindset that glorifies constant productivity, relentless ambition, and the belief that success should be pursued through uninterrupted effort, regardless of personal cost. Although it is often associated with modern work culture and social media, the idea has broader psychological significance because it encourages people to treat exhaustion, busyness, and overwork as signs of discipline or personal worth. In this sense, hustle culture is not simply about working hard. It is more accurately understood as a value system that rewards speed, output, and performative productivity while discouraging limits, recovery, and balance.

At the same time, hustle culture is not merely a professional attitude. Commentators and psychology sources suggest that it can shape identity, self-esteem, and well-being by making people feel that they must always be achieving more in order to remain worthy or productive. However, its effects can be damaging when effort becomes disconnected from meaning, rest is treated as weakness, and stress is normalised rather than questioned. For that reason, hustle culture has become an important concept in discussions of burnout, work-related pressure, and the growing pushback against lifestyles organised around constant performance.

Slow Living
Lifestyle & Mindfulness

The term slow living is commonly used to describe a way of life that values intention, simplicity, and a more mindful relationship with time, rather than constant speed, productivity, or overstimulation. Although it is often associated with lifestyle culture, the idea has broader psychological significance because it reflects an effort to slow daily rhythms and pay closer attention to everyday experience. In this sense, slow living is not simply about doing everything at a lower speed. It is more accurately understood as a deliberate resistance to hurry, excess, and the pressure to treat busyness as a measure of worth.

At the same time, slow living is not the same as inactivity or withdrawal from responsibility. Commentators and mental-health sources suggest that it can function as a way of restoring attention, reducing overload, and creating more space for reflection, presence, and meaningful routines. However, its value depends on how it is practised. If it becomes only an aesthetic ideal, it may remain superficial; if treated as a genuine shift in priorities, it can support well-being and a more sustainable relationship with work, consumption, and everyday life. For that reason, slow living has become an important concept in discussions of mindfulness, simplicity, and the search for balance in a culture shaped by speed and acceleration.

Aura Farming
Identity & Social Media

The expression aura farming is used to describe the deliberate cultivation of an impressive, attractive, or charismatic public image, often through behaviour that appears effortless, self-assured, or subtly mysterious. Although the term is strongly associated with internet culture, its broader significance lies in the way identity can be strategically performed for public consumption. Social media has made this process especially visible, since users can shape how they are perceived through carefully chosen images, gestures, captions, and attitudes that communicate confidence or coolness without seeming overtly performative.

What makes aura farming particularly interesting is that it depends on suggestion rather than direct self-promotion: the ideal image is not announced too loudly, but implied. For that reason, the practice reflects a wider shift in online culture, where status may be built less through explicit achievement than through the successful projection of style, composure, and symbolic capital. Critics might argue that this blurs the line between authenticity and performance, encouraging people to treat personality itself as something to be designed, displayed, and continuously managed.

Vibe Coding
Technology & AI

The phrase vibe coding has emerged to describe a new style of software creation in which people use natural-language prompts to guide artificial intelligence systems in writing code. The term captures a shift away from traditional line-by-line programming toward a more conversational, intuition-driven workflow. In this model, the user does not necessarily control every detail of the code directly, but instead describes intentions, tests outputs, and refines results through repeated interaction with an AI tool.

Supporters see this as a democratising development because it lowers technical barriers and allows both experienced programmers and newcomers to build digital products more quickly. At the same time, critics point out that such convenience can create overconfidence, shallow understanding, and new risks related to security, debugging, and software quality. Vibe coding therefore represents more than a passing buzzword: it signals a broader transformation in how programming is imagined, who gets to participate in it, and what counts as technical expertise in an AI-assisted era.

Bluewashing
Corporate Ethics

The term bluewashing is commonly used to describe situations in which companies present themselves as socially responsible or strongly committed to human rights by associating with respected international frameworks, while their actual practices may fall far short of those ideals. Critics have used it to describe cases in which firms appear to gain reputational benefits from affiliation without making meaningful changes to labour conditions, governance, or broader human rights performance.

Because social responsibility is harder to measure than financial results, public claims about ethical commitment can be especially powerful as image-management tools. A company may highlight partnerships and carefully framed reports in order to signal moral credibility to investors, consumers, and regulators, even when evidence of concrete reform remains limited. Scholars have warned that this kind of communication can distort public understanding, creating the impression that accountability mechanisms are working when symbolic commitment has in fact replaced substantive change. Critics therefore argue that closer attention should be paid to transparent reporting and genuine improvement, not to the language of responsibility alone.

Pinkwashing
Brand Identity & Social Justice

The term pinkwashing, sometimes also called rainbow-washing, is widely used to describe the practice of publicly aligning with LGBTQ+ inclusion in ways that are highly visible but not always deeply substantive. In commercial settings, this often appears through Pride-themed campaigns, rainbow-branded merchandise, and marketing messages that present companies as progressive and socially responsible. Such gestures can certainly increase visibility, and in some cases they do reflect meaningful support. However, critics argue that the problem arises when symbolic inclusion is not matched by consistent action, such as fair employment practices or long-term advocacy.

In these situations, inclusion becomes less a lived commitment than a carefully managed brand identity. The concern is that highly polished messaging may encourage the public to equate visibility with justice, even when underlying structures remain unchanged. As a result, pinkwashing is often criticised not simply for commercial opportunism, but for turning a social struggle into a reputational asset. Many observers therefore insist that genuine allyship should be measured not by seasonal campaigns alone, but by sustained conduct, institutional choices, and the willingness to bear real costs in support of equality.

Healthwashing
Food Marketing & Consumer Behaviour

Healthwashing refers to the practice of making products, especially foods and beverages, appear healthier than they really are through selective language, imagery, and marketing cues. In an era when consumers are increasingly concerned with nutrition and preventive health, brands have strong incentives to align themselves with these values. They may highlight natural ingredients, reduced-fat claims, or words associated with vitality, even when the overall product remains high in sugar, heavily processed, or nutritionally questionable.

Research on food marketing has shown that health-related cues can shape perception in powerful ways, leading consumers to infer benefits that are not fully supported by the product's actual composition. This is precisely what makes healthwashing so effective: it does not always rely on outright falsehood, but often on emphasis, omission, and the strategic framing of information. Critics argue that such practices exploit people's desire to make responsible choices for themselves and their families, and can weaken public health efforts by creating a misleading sense of nutritional value. Experts therefore stress that the language of health is not always the same as genuine healthfulness.

Reading 13
AI-Washing
Technology & Corporate Deception

Contemporary commerce is driven by the rapid commercialisation of Artificial Intelligence. Utilising sophisticated language models and neural networks, cutting-edge systems can process vast datasets into actionable insights with astonishing precision. However, within this landscape, the practice of AI-washing has emerged as a significant concern. The fundamental mechanics of this corporate deception rely on strategic marketing rather than actual machine learning: companies capture popular tech buzzwords, while superficial rules-based software attempts to masquerade as genuine AI.

The economic ramifications of deceptive tech marketing extend far beyond consumer disappointment. Within the largely unregulated software industry, AI-washing threatens to destabilize critical markets by harvesting unearned capital through exaggerated technical capabilities. Addressing this challenge necessitates statutory frameworks that clearly define what constitutes true AI, though the intangible nature of algorithmic decision-making often complicates legal definitions. Fundamentally, fostering critical tech-literacy among the public remains the most formidable defence against algorithmic exploitation.

Reading 14
Artwashing
Culture & Urban Power

Artwashing refers to the use of art, artists, or cultural activity to soften the image of controversial developments, institutions, or sources of wealth. The term is especially common in debates about urban redevelopment, where galleries, installations, murals, and creative branding can make an area appear vibrant, desirable, and culturally alive. Yet critics argue that cultural prestige can also be instrumentalised to mask more troubling processes, particularly gentrification, displacement, or socially unequal redevelopment.

In this sense, art may help repackage a place or institution in ways that attract investment and approval while making underlying tensions less visible. More recent scholarship suggests that artwashing can operate through developers, local authorities, and even artists themselves when cultural projects become part of a broader process of rebranding and social cleansing. The central concern is not that art lacks value, but that its symbolic legitimacy can be used to obscure who gains, who loses, and whose voice is marginalised. For that reason, critics contend that cultural projects should be assessed not only for their aesthetic appeal, but also for their political and economic context.

Reading 15
Sportswashing
Sport & Geopolitics

Sportswashing is a term commonly used to describe the strategic use of sport to improve the reputation of a state, corporation, or political actor whose record may otherwise attract criticism. Because sport is associated with excellence, unity, and international prestige, it offers an unusually powerful platform for image-building. Governments and business interests can sponsor major clubs, host prestigious tournaments, or align themselves with famous athletes in order to project modernity, ambition, and legitimacy before a global audience.

Yet critics argue that such visibility can also divert attention from troubling realities, including human rights concerns, political repression, or involvement in regional conflict. In this way, the spectacle of sport may function not only as entertainment but also as reputational insulation. The concern is not that sport itself is harmful, but that its emotional appeal can make difficult questions easier to ignore. For that reason, many observers contend that fans, clubs, sponsors, and governing bodies should look beyond branding and ask who benefits politically when sport becomes intertwined with national image management.
